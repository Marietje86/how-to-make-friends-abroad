A note from Mari
When I moved abroad, I underestimated one thing completely: how disorienting it is to have no one who knows your name yet. I had the apartment, the plan, the excitement. What I didn't have was someone to call when the novelty wore off at 9pm on a Tuesday.
Loneliness abroad isn't about being alone. It's about being surrounded by a city that doesn't know you exist yet. The antidote isn't waiting to be found - it's creating small, repeated moments of contact until something sticks.
This guide covers what I wish I'd known in my first weeks: where the right people actually are, how to start something from nothing, and how to build the routines that turn acquaintances into your people.

Section 02 · How to Approach
The anatomy of a first conversation
The 5-step approach
From eye contact to plans made
1
Position yourself
Sit near people, not away from them. The side of the coffee shop closest to the counter. The bench nearest the group activity. Proximity makes everything easier.
2
Use the environment, not yourself
Comment on something present: the class, the event, the café, the view. "Is this your first time at this group?" beats any compliment-based opener. The room gives you the reason.
3
Ask one question and actually listen
Most people monologue. The person who listens and asks a follow-up question stands out completely. Ask one thing. Wait for the whole answer. Ask about the answer.
4
Disclose something small
"I just moved here last month" or "I'm still figuring out the neighborhood" - honest small disclosures invite reciprocal openness. People bond over vulnerability faster than over impressiveness.
5
Make a specific invitation before you leave
Not "we should hang out sometime." That dies. Instead: "I'm going back to this market on Saturday - want to come?" Specificity is what turns a conversation into a relationship.
The mindset shift that changes everything
Stop trying to be interesting. Try to be interested.
In a new city, the instinct is to prove yourself - your life, your backstory, your reasons for being there. Resist it. The people who make friends fast aren't the most interesting. They're the most genuinely curious. Ask more than you share, at least in the first conversation.
You don't need to be liked by everyone. You need three people in the same city who are glad you exist. Start there.
Common mistakes
What doesn't work (and why)
Waiting for people to approach you first +
Everyone in a new city is waiting for someone else to go first. In a room of introverts, the extrovert wins by default - not because they're more confident, but because they moved first. Move first.
Only spending time with other expats +
Expat communities are a lifeline early on. But a life built entirely within expat circles can create a bubble that delays integration and shrinks over time as people leave. Aim for a mix - expat and local connections both.
Giving up after one awkward encounter +
One bad interaction is data, not verdict. Social dynamics in a new country take longer than anywhere else because you're adapting to unfamiliar social cues, humor registers, and communication norms simultaneously. Keep going.
Treating every interaction as high stakes +
Not every conversation needs to become a friendship. Some are just practice reps. The goal of early social contact is volume - you're building the muscle, not landing the relationship. Go wide first, then deepen.

Why these work
The anatomy of a good opener
Every effective starter does two things: it's specific to the shared context (not generic), and it invites a response that's longer than one word. "Do you like it here?" dies. "How long have you been coming to this class?" lives - it gives the other person something real to say, and what they say tells you whether there's a conversation worth having.
The best openers aren't witty. They're warm and they're specific. Anyone can use them. You don't need to be "good at talking to people" first.

Strategy
How to use apps without wasting time on them
Apps are the bridge, not the destination. The goal is to move someone from app → first meetup → recurring activity → real friendship, as quickly as possible. One week from first message to first meeting is a good target. Anything longer and the connection usually fades.
Every app connection that stays only digital is a connection that doesn't actually count. The goal is the coffee, not the chat.

Section 05 · Connection Routines
How consistency builds a social life
Week by week
The 30-day connection rhythm
The science behind it
Why routine creates connection faster than effort
Proximity plus repetition is the oldest friendship formula there is. Research on how friendships form (including Robert Dunbar's work on social bonding) consistently shows that repeated, low-stakes contact builds closeness faster than occasional high-effort gestures. You don't need to be charming. You need to show up to the same place twice.
Your regular coffee shop isn't just a place to work. It's infrastructure. Every week you go, you're depositing into a social account that will eventually pay out.
The core rule
Pick one recurring thing. Protect it like a job.
You don't need six activities. You need one weekly thing that happens whether you feel social or not. A language class, a run club, a yoga studio, a volunteer shift. The consistency is the strategy. Show up when you feel like it and when you don't - the relationship compounds over time.
Section 06 · Avoiding Isolation
Recognizing and breaking the spiral
Signal vs Response
The isolation spiral - and how to interrupt it
Warning signals
●Cancelling plans you made with yourself
●Default evenings: phone, delivery, streaming
●Feeling like "everyone else has their people"
●Declining invitations because it feels like effort
●Telling yourself you'll start being social "next week"
●Only talking to people from home
●Measuring your social life against how it was back home
Pattern interrupts
●Leave the apartment for 30 minutes with no agenda
●Text one person - any person - something real
●Say yes to the next invitation, whatever it is
●Go somewhere with people, not to meet people
●Do the one recurring thing, even for 20 minutes
●Call someone from home - connection doesn't have to be local
●Write: what did life look like the last time I felt connected?
The hardest truth
The spiral is not a character flaw. It's a timing problem.
Isolation abroad almost always happens in weeks 4–8: the novelty has worn off, the logistics are settled, and the reality that this city doesn't know you yet sets in. This is the moment most people interpret as "I'm not the kind of person who can do this." They're wrong. It's the inflection point - and the people who push through it are the ones who build extraordinary lives.
The moment it feels hardest is almost never the moment to give up. It's the moment just before it gets easier.
